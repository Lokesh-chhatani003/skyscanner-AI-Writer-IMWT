[1.1 Playful] Our tone is playful. We write with a light, natural energy. Playfulness helps us feel human and builds trust – but it must never reduce clarity.

[1.1 Playful] Note: Avoid adding or omitting texts that would mislead the final user.

[1.1 Playful] Use these tools when they feel natural and keep meaning clear (and when they work in your language):

[1.1 Playful] Use these tools sparingly and only if they localise well:

[1.1 Playful] Always choose clarity over wordplay. If a device is hard to adapt or sounds unnatural in your language, do not use it.

[1.2 Geeky] Keep the tone enthusiastic but controlled. It should feel like a knowledgeable friend, not a lecture.

[1.3 Helpful] Helpful is our foundation. If you must choose between playful, geeky and helpful, be helpful first.

[1.3 Helpful] Focus on what they need to know, not on us.

[1.3 Helpful] Start with the benefit or instruction, then explain how we know.

[1.3 Helpful] Always apologise if something is our fault. If it isn’t, show empathy instead.

[1.3 Helpful] Avoid cold phrases like “We apologise for the inconvenience.

[1.3 Helpful] Use short sentences and simple structures.

[URL requirements] No spaces - If you need to separate words, use a standard hyphen -.

[Placeholders, variables and tags] Placeholders, variables and tags are there to make the text dynamic (e.g. inserting a city, date or price) or to format it. They must not be translated, deleted or changed.

[Placeholders, variables and tags] Always keep them exactly as in the source, placed in the correct order, and make sure the sentence is still natural and grammatically correct in your language. Make sure to insert spaces where needed before/after the tags to avoid words being glued together when the placeholders are populated.

[Inclusive language] Our language should be inclusive, welcoming and respectful to all travellers. When translating for Skyscanner, avoid stereotypes, unnecessary references to gender, family, age, ability, or background, and choose wording that includes as many people as possible. Always prefer neutral, precise terms over clichés or labels.

[Inclusive language] Use gender-neutral language where possible.

[Inclusive language] Do not assume who people travel with, how they look, or what they can do.

[Inclusive language] Avoid jokes or idioms that rely on stereotypes about nationality, age, gender, religion, or any other group.

[Inclusive language] Focus on travellers’ needs and situations, not on labels.

[Punctuation and spacing] It is extremely important to use punctuation marks (علامات الترقيم) correctly in Arabic, which means you should not follow the source punctuation blindly. It should be noted that some punctuation marks in Arabic look different from the English counterparts, e.g. the English comma is (,) while the Arabic comma (الفاصلة) points the opposite way (،) and it is written on top of the line. The same applies to the question mark, the Arabic question mark (علامة الاستفهام) should be written like this (؟).

[Commas] Exclamation marks (!) are often used in English with words such as “sorry”, “warning”, “important”, etc. However, this is not the case in Arabic, as it is used more often to express surprise or amazement.  So do not overuse exclamation marks. A comma, a colon, or a period can be used instead, depending on context.

[Hyphens and Dashes] The use of hyphens and dashes differs significantly from English. Arabic does not typically use hyphens and dashes to join words or to break words at the end of a line.

[Hyphens and Dashes] Here is how hyphens and dashes function in Arabic:

[Hyphens and Dashes] Arabic uses the dash (—) or hyphen-dash (-) in specific structural ways rather than for word-building:

[Hyphens and Dashes] Dialogue: A hyphen is used at the beginning of a line to indicate a change in speaker in a conversation. For example:

[Hyphens and Dashes] —  هل يمكنني تغيير موعد رحلتي عبر التطبيق؟
— نعم، يمكنك ذلك من خلال قسم "إدارة الحجوزات".

[Hyphens and Dashes] Numbers: Used to separate a number from the text in a list (e.g., 1- الأول) or to indicate a range (e.g., 18-28).

[Emphasis and intensifiers] Sometimes we use formatting and wording to emphasise key information for it to be easy to spot, or as an intensifier for a part of the message.

[Emphasis and intensifiers] We use the quotation marks (") in Arabic to distinguish the key information, such as the UI texts, proper names, speech, etc. The bold or italic formatting should be followed as per the source or any specific instructions.

[Emphasis and intensifiers] Since the Arabic script does not use capital letters, these formatting tools and punctuation marks are essential for providing visual clarity and emphasis within a written text.

[References] When you refer to other content (pages, features, sections, links), use clear, consistent names and, where relevant, the exact label used in the UI. We use the quotation marks (") in Arabic to highlight such content.

[Titles and CTAs] When formatting titles and CTAs in Arabic, we focus on using quotation marks, bold, an appropriate font size, and clear language instead of non-existent capitalization rules.

[Titles and CTAs] As a rule of thumb, we avoid title case and prefer sentence case, unless the language’s standard rules say otherwise, except for some Marketing and Ads content.

[Months and days] We use the Arabized names (derived from Latin) rather than the Levantine/Syriac names. This ensures consistency across different regions.

[Glossary terms / product names] Glossary terms and product feature names should follow a consistent capitalisation pattern across the product. Use sentence case for descriptive unless they are treated as branded names in your language.

[Glossary terms / product names] Keep capitalisation the same in UI and running copy, and do not switch between different styles (for example, avoid mixing “Price Alerts” and “price alerts” without a clear rule).

[The use of Anglicisms] Avoid unnecessary Anglicisms if there is a clear, natural equivalent in the target language. Only keep English words when they are widely used and expected by travellers (for example, brand names or common digital terms). Make sure any English term you keep fits the tone and formality of your market and stays consistent across the product.

[Abbreviations and acronyms] Use abbreviations and acronyms only when they are widely understood by travellers in your market (for example: airport codes, well-known transport terms). If there is a risk of confusion, write the full term on first mention and add the abbreviation in brackets. Avoid creating new abbreviations just to save space.

[Addressing our users – Formality and T/V policy] How we address users (formal vs informal “you”) must follow the norms and expectations of each language and market. In general, we aim for a friendly, respectful tone that feels like a helpful travel pal, not overly formal or distant. Arabic does not have a true T/V distinction. Instead, formality and politeness are conveyed using different pronouns and specific polite phrases.

[Addressing our users – Formality and T/V policy] Avoid switching between forms unless there is a clear, documented exception (for example, specific legal text that must use a different level of formality).

[CTAs and active voice] Use the active voice wherever possible, especially in headlines and CTAs. The active voice is usually clearer, and it sounds more like a direct conversation with our users.

[CTAs and active voice] CTAs should be short, direct and written in the active voice.

[CTAs and active voice] The imperative verbal format is to be used for CTAs in Arabic, e.g. سجِّل الدخول and احجز الآن.

[CTAs and active voice] Focus on what the user can do next, using clear verbs and simple structures.

[CTAs and active voice] Avoid long or vague phrases and avoid passive constructions where the user is not the clear subject.

[CTAs and active voice] When adapting to each language, keep CTAs natural and action-oriented, in line with local expectations for apps and websites.

[Transliteration] Follow common local usage and official standards when transliterating place names, stations, and key travel terms. Be consistent within the language: don’t mix different transliteration systems in the same text. Do not invent new spellings or “localise” brand names that are normally kept in Latin script. If there is an established local exonym (well-known local version of a place name), use that; otherwise use the standard transliteration.

[Geo-terms] A toponym is the general name for any place or geographical entity. They are used to refer to a variety of different places.

[Geo-terms] The main aim of translating toponyms is to lead the user to the correct geographical location. For this reason, the translation of a toponym should never be vague, confusing, or misleading. So please do thorough research before translating the terms.

[Geo-terms] When dealing with location names, we will come across two instances:

[Geo-terms] When the toponym is known and popular:

[Geo-terms] In this case, we can translate to the commonly known version.

[Geo-terms] When the toponym is unknown:

[Geo-terms] Please keep it untranslated.

[Geo-terms] Please note that in some cases you might find that a toponym has both a translated version and a transliterated version. In that case, please use the term that yields more search results. For example:

[Geo-terms] Time Square: it has a translated version ميدان التايمز and a transliterated version تايمز سكوير.

[Geo-terms] A simple Google search will show you which term is used the most (in that case, it is the translated version), and we use that.

[Geo-terms] The most important thing is to keep the user in mind: they should be able to look up the place name easily and/or ask for directions if needed. Always stick to the most commonly used and understandable version of a name.

[Geo-terms] How to translate city names

[Geo-terms] Do not translate a city name unless there is a commonly known translation for it. If you can’t find the city name in Arabic, please keep it exactly as written in the source, including capitalization, accents, or any other diacritical marks. This is important because the toponym needs to be searchable, and the user needs to have accurate content to do so.

[Geo-terms] If there is a translation for the historical site in Arabic, we should use that. For example:

[Geo-terms] Great Wall of China is سور الصين العظيم

[Geo-terms] Roman Colosseum is الكولوسيوم الروماني

[Geo-terms] If there is no Arabic translation but the historical site is still well-known by its original name, we use the transliterated version. For example:

[Geo-terms] If the site is unknown and doesn’t yield results in Arabic (translated/transliterated) we keep it as the source. And if the unknown POIs contain commonly used descriptors such as museum, park, theatre, botanic, etc., we translate the descriptors keeping the unknown historical site untranslated.

[Geo-terms] Beaches, vineyards, surf spots, and golf courses

[Geo-terms] These are points of interest too, and, in general, the same approach should be applied here as with similar toponyms.

[Geo-terms] Again, Wikipedia, Google search, and Google Maps are the main sources of reference.

[Airport names] When translating airport names, refer to the official sources to make sure the name is correct.

[Airport names] Handling airport names involves a mix of translation (for descriptive meaning) and transliteration (for proper nouns) to ensure accuracy and consistency.

[Airport names] Proper Nouns: These are transliterated into the target language script to maintain their original identity and pronunciation. For example, the city name "Miami" is written as "ميامي" in Arabic.

[Airport names] Descriptive Words: Words that describe the type or location of the airport are translated into the target language. For example:

[Airport names] "Airport" is translated as "مطار" in Arabic.

[Airport names] "International" is translated as "دولي".

[Airport names] "Regional" is translated as "إقليمي".

[Airport names] Consistency and International Standards: Organizations like IATA and ICAO use specific standards and unified three- or four-letter codes (e.g., CAI for القاهرة or JED for جدة) to avoid confusion that might arise from different translations.

[Airport names] Precedence for Common Names: Sometimes, the widely recognized English name for an airport is used even if it differs from the local name to facilitate international communication.

[Airport names] When translating "Miami International Airport" into Arabic:

[Airport names] "Airport" is translated to "مطار".

[Airport names] "Miami" is transliterated to "ميامي".

[Airport names] "International" is translated to "دولي".

[Airport names] The complete name becomes: مطار ميامي الدولي.

[Airport names] Never add the word “airport” when translating an airport name to avoid duplication; it’s usually used to populate placeholders in a running copy, and the term “airport” is added to the copy when needed.

[Emojis and symbols] Use emojis and symbols sparingly and only where they support clarity or add a light, on-brand touch.

[Emojis and symbols] Avoid replacing key words with emojis, and never use emojis in legal, policy or serious problem/incident messages.

[Use of Skyscanner brand] The name Skyscanner is a core part of our brand identity and must be treated consistently across all languages. It should remain recognisable, correctly spelled, and used in a way that feels natural to the local audience while still protecting the brand.

[Use of Skyscanner brand] Never translate or adapt the meaning of the name (e.g. never translate “sky” or “scanner”).

[Use of Skyscanner brand] Do not add articles unless they are required in your language and sound natural.

[Use of Skyscanner brand] If in doubt, always use Skyscanner in English and flag questions to Skyscanner.

[Feature naming] Avoid direct, word-for-word translations if they sound strange, misleading, or carry unintended meanings. Only use the English name if it is already widely known and expected in your market.

[Feature naming] Example:

The English term “Packages” may be misunderstood as “parcels” in some languages, while we refer to a new vertical offering package holidays including flights and a hotel.
A clearer, more natural option in your language could be “package holidays” or the local equivalent travellers recognise. We can use عرض or حزمة as a translation of package.

[The use of “Best”] Be careful with the use of “best” and superlatives in general. We are unable to claim that we can find travellers the “best prices” and “best deals” as this is an objective claim versus our competition that we would not be able to substantiate.

[The use of “Best”] We can, however, use “Best” when there is a clear disclaimer explaining how "best" is measured.

[The use of “Best”] For example, we can sort our flight results by “Best” results since we have a disclaimer clearly explaining how this is measured:

[The use of “Best”] Generally, when the English copy says “best”, it’s safe to translate it the same way. When this is not the case, do not use “best” or other superlatives. Instead, use subjective claims such as “epic prices” or “amazing deals”.

[The use of “Discount”] Skyscanner never offer discounts to users. Any discounts come from partners (for example Trip.com), not by Skyscanner.

[The use of “Discount”] For this and especially for legal reasons we can therefore never imply that “we offer” or “Skyscanner offers” a discount. This must be translated in a neutral way, i.e. “You are offered a discount”.

[The use of “Discount”] Never use the translation or equivalent of “discount” when the English source refers to a “deal” or “price drops” – these are not discounts but results of price fluctuation and it would be legally incorrect to refer to discounts.

[Translating sustainability-related content] When localising content about anything related to sustainability for Skyscanner, it’s essential to communicate responsibly and avoid greenwashing. Travel has an undeniable environmental impact, and our language must reflect this honestly and transparently. Use the following principles when translating or adapting sustainability-related content:

[Translating sustainability-related content] Prefer comparatives over absolutes. Use relative statements such as “X is more sustainable than Y”, rather than implying that any option is fully sustainable.

[Translating sustainability-related content] Avoid superlatives. Do not use expressions like “the most ecological flight” or “the greenest option”, as they can overstate environmental benefits.
